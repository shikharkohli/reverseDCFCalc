4. Assumptions used in this model

Every reverse DCF bakes in modelling choices. These are ours — read them before trusting the output.

  • Base FCF = average of the last 3 annual columns of Cash from Operating Activity − |Cash from Investing Activity|. We use investing cash flow as a capex proxy, which also captures acquisitions and strategic investments — not just maintenance capex.
  • Discount rate (r) is user-set (default 12%) as a proxy for Indian equity cost of capital. There is no single "right" value — adjust it for risk.
  • Constant growth is assumed during the explicit projection period. Real businesses grow in fits and starts.
  • Terminal value uses the Gordon Growth Model applied at year N. This single number often drives >60% of intrinsic value.
  • Equity DCF — we compare the present value of FCFs directly to market capitalisation. This implicitly assumes capital structure (debt/equity mix) stays roughly constant.
  • No dilution or buybacks during the projection window. Share count is taken from the META block as reported.
  • Tax rate is whatever the reported P&L implies — no normalisation for tax holidays or one-offs.
  • All figures in ₹ crore as reported by Screener.in. Consolidated vs standalone depends on the export chosen.
  • Investibility bands are GDP-anchored heuristics: <8% Investible, 8–13% Fair, 13–18% Expensive, ≥18% Overvalued. They are not investment advice.

5. Limitations of DCF analysis

  1. Garbage in, garbage out. Historical CFO can be distorted by working-capital swings, one-offs, or aggressive accounting. If the inputs are noisy, the output is meaningless.
  2. Terminal value dominates. Typically more than 60% of intrinsic value sits in the Gordon terminal. Small changes in r or gT swing the answer dramatically.
  3. Constant-growth fiction. Real companies face S-curves, disruption, cyclicality and competitive decay. Linear compounding is a convenient lie.
  4. Discount rate is arbitrary. There is no "correct" cost of capital; different analysts can justify 10% or 14% for the same business.
  5. Not suitable for banks, NBFCs and insurers (cash flow is not the value driver), early-stage or loss-making companies, and deep cyclicals observed at trough or peak earnings.
  6. Ignores optionality. New business lines, M&A potential, capital-allocation skill and moat evolution are not captured.
  7. Break-even, not a buy signal. "Implied growth" tells you what the market expects — it does not build in any margin of safety.
  8. Point-in-time. The model reflects the latest reported financials. Forward guidance, management commentary or post-reporting-date events are not in it.
  9. Screener data caveats. Consolidated vs standalone mismatches, restated numbers, missing segments and rounding all affect the output.
